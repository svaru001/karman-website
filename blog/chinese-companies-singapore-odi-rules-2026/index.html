Chinese Companies Expanding Through Singapore: What China's New 2026 Outbound Investment Rules Mean

📅 Published 25 Nov 2026 ⏱ 11 min read ✍️ Karman Editorial Team

Quick answer

China's first State Council regulation on outbound investment (Order No. 837) took effect on 1 July 2026, and the NDRC's new implementing Measures (Order No. 49) take effect on 1 December 2026. Chinese companies can still set up and expand through Singapore, but the investment must be filed with (or, for sensitive projects, approved by) the NDRC and MOFCOM, with foreign exchange registered through a bank. Resident individuals are now covered too. Holding the shares through Hong Kong or another offshore company does not take the deal outside the rules, because the NDRC looks at substance over form. The practical answer is a properly filed Singapore company with real management, staff and operations in Singapore.

  • Order 837 (effective 1 July 2026) is China's first State Council-level rule on outbound investment.
  • NDRC Order 49 replaces the 2017 rules from 1 December 2026, with no transition period for deals that straddle that date.
  • Resident individuals now have an express NDRC filing route. Filing does not remove foreign exchange limits.
  • Deals of US$100m or more need a preliminary report at least 10 working days before signing.
  • Fines of 0.1% to 1% of the Chinese investment amount, plus possible 1-3 year bans on outbound investment.
  • Singapore still works as a launchpad, but only with real substance: management, people and decisions in Singapore.

For Chinese founders "going global" (出海), Singapore has been the first stop for years. China's Ministry of Commerce data, as reported by The Straits Times, shows Chinese direct investment into Singapore rising from US$8.3 billion in 2022 to US$13.09 billion in 2023 and US$17.89 billion in 2024, making Singapore the largest destination for Chinese investment in Asia outside Hong Kong.

2026 changed the rules on the Chinese side. A new State Council regulation took effect on 1 July 2026, and the NDRC's rewritten implementing Measures take effect on 1 December 2026. This guide explains what changed, who is affected, and how to set up a Singapore company that works under the new regime.

The key changes under NDRC Order 49

1. Individuals are now in scope

Before, a PRC resident individual investing overseas directly sat largely outside NDRC rules. The main regime was SAFE's 2014 Circular 37, which covers round-trip investment through offshore special purpose vehicles. Under the new Measures, individuals file with the provincial development and reform commission where their household is registered or where they usually live, regardless of the amount. Law firms note that filing does not remove foreign exchange restrictions, and SAFE has not yet issued the matching rules for individuals' outbound payments.

2. Substance over form

The Measures expressly look at substance over legal form when deciding who the investor is, whether there is control, and how much Chinese money is involved. The "Chinese investment amount" includes capital, loans and guarantees, whether given directly or through controlled offshore entities, and now expressly includes data alongside technology and IP. As Freshfields puts it, offshore funding or holding structures do not by themselves take a deal outside NDRC oversight.

3. Earlier reporting for large or sensitive deals

Where the Chinese investment is US$100 million or more, or the project could affect China's diplomatic relations with the host country, the investor must file a preliminary work report at least 10 working days before key steps such as signing an investment agreement. It is a notification, not an approval, but it must go in before signing.

4. Reinvestment by your offshore companies

If your Singapore company (controlled from China) invests further, for example into Indonesia, Vietnam or back into China, that reinvestment must generally be reported at least 10 working days before it happens. The old US$300 million reporting threshold for reinvestment has been removed.

5. Tighter reporting after the deal

  • Completion or termination report: within 10 working days (previously 20).
  • Major adverse events, including foreign demands to hand over technology, data or assets: report immediately.
  • Annual report on overseas investments: by 30 June each year.

6. Heavier penalties

Investing without the required approval or filing, or filing false information, can lead to confiscation of gains and a fine of 0.1% to 0.5% of the Chinese investment amount, rising to 0.5% to 1% and an order to dispose of the investment if not corrected. Responsible individuals face personal fines of RMB 20,000 to 50,000, and investors can be barred from outbound investment for one to three years.

Normal filings are processed within about 7 working days of formal acceptance. MOFCOM filing and the bank's ODI foreign exchange registration are separate steps that still apply. A national security review can also apply, separately from filing. Commentators expect AI, semiconductors, batteries, EVs, robotics and quantum technology to be treated with the most caution, though no official list of these has been published.

The end of "relocate to Singapore, then sell"

In April 2026, Beijing blocked Meta's acquisition of Manus, an AI start-up founded by Chinese entrepreneurs and headquartered in Singapore. Commentators saw it as the end of a playbook where a Chinese team re-domiciles to Singapore, raises foreign money and sells to a US buyer without engaging Chinese regulators. Order 49's substance-over-form rule and its coverage of data and technology contributions write that approach into law.

The flip side is that the rules are not designed to stop Chinese companies going abroad. Reporting by The Straits Times suggests they will reshape how Chinese firms expand through Singapore rather than deter them, with slower timelines for sensitive sectors.

Why Singapore still makes sense

  • A neutral, rules-based base with strong rule of law and courts trusted by Western and Asian counterparties.
  • Tax: 17% corporate tax with start-up and partial exemptions, no capital gains tax in most cases, no tax on dividends paid to shareholders, and a double tax agreement with China. See holding company tax benefits.
  • Access to ASEAN: a base for Indonesia, Vietnam, Thailand and Malaysia, with Singapore's free trade agreements including RCEP.
  • Trade: a US tariff rate on Singapore-origin goods below most of the region. Origin must be genuine. See US tariffs and Singapore companies.
  • Capital: banks, family offices and investors who are comfortable with Chinese-founded companies that are properly structured.

Substance: what "real" looks like in Singapore

Both Chinese regulators and Western counterparties now look past the certificate of incorporation. As one Singapore investor told The Straits Times, incorporation alone is not enough. What matters is where talent is based, where data comes from and where decisions are made. In practice that means:

  1. Management in Singapore: at least one director ordinarily resident in Singapore (required by law) and, ideally, senior managers on Employment Passes making real decisions here. Board meetings should be held in Singapore.
  2. Local hires and premises: a genuine office and Singapore-based staff for regional sales, finance or product.
  3. Clear data and IP lines: document which IP and data the Singapore company owns, licenses or develops. Transfers of technology or data from China may need their own approvals.
  4. Transfer pricing: arm's length pricing and documentation for dealings with the Chinese parent. See our transfer pricing simulator.
  5. Tax residence: to claim treaty benefits the company needs a Singapore Certificate of Residence, which requires control and management in Singapore.

A compliant set-up sequence

  1. Structure first: decide whether the Singapore company sits under the Chinese parent, under a founder directly (now an individual filing), or under an existing offshore holding company. All three routes now involve NDRC reporting.
  2. PRC filings: complete NDRC filing (or approval), MOFCOM filing and bank FX registration before funds leave China. Make valid filing a condition of any third-party investment.
  3. Incorporate in Singapore: name check, local resident director, registered address, constitution, then incorporation on BizFile, usually within a day. See how to register on BizFile.
  4. Bank account: expect enhanced due diligence on source of funds. Your ODI paperwork is exactly what the bank will ask for.
  5. People: apply for Employment Passes under COMPASS for transferred managers. Try our COMPASS calculator.
  6. Ongoing: Singapore annual filings plus Chinese annual reports (by 30 June), reinvestment reports and completion reports.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Wiring capital from personal accounts or through third parties to "save time". This breaches FX rules and now NDRC rules, and banks in Singapore will ask how the money left China.
  • Assuming a Hong Kong or BVI holding company avoids filing. Under substance over form, it does not.
  • Signing a term sheet or investment agreement on a US$100m+ deal before the preliminary report has been filed for 10 working days.
  • Moving source code, models or customer data to the Singapore company without checking Chinese export control and data rules.
  • A nominee-only Singapore presence. It fails Chinese, Singapore and counterparty substance tests at the same time.
