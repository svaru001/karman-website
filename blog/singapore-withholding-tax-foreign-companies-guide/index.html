Singapore Withholding Tax on Payments to Foreign Companies (2026): Rates, Treaty Table and Examples

📅 Published 16 Nov 2026 ⏱ 10 min read ✍️ Karman Editorial Team

Quick answer

Singapore withholding tax is deducted by a Singapore payer from certain payments to non-residents: 15% on interest, 10% on royalties, 15% on equipment rent and 17% on service fees for work done in Singapore. Services performed wholly outside Singapore are not subject to it, dividends are exempt, and tax treaties with around 100 countries can reduce the rates. Form IR37 is due by the 15th of the second month after payment.

Key takeaways

  • Services done wholly outside Singapore: no withholding tax.
  • No treaty: interest 15%, royalties 10%, onsite services 17%, non-resident directors 24%.
  • Treaty examples: UAE interest 0%, UK interest 5%, Malaysia royalties 8%, Netherlands royalties 0%. No US or Hong Kong treaty.
  • You need the payee's Certificate of Residence to apply a treaty rate, and must still file IR37.
  • Late payment: 5% penalty, plus 1% a month up to another 15%.

Search Google from Mumbai, Kuala Lumpur, Jakarta, London or Sydney for "Singapore withholding tax" and the suggestions are the same everywhere: on services, on payments to foreign companies, rate, DTA, calculator. Foreign suppliers want to know why their Singapore client paid them less than the invoice. Singapore companies want to know whether they owe IRAS money every time they pay an overseas freelancer, parent company or software vendor.

The short version: many cross-border payments are not subject to Singapore withholding tax at all, a tax treaty often cuts the rate, and the payer is the one who gets penalised for getting it wrong. This guide covers the rates, the treaty table, the services rule most people miss, and three worked examples. To run your own numbers, use our free Singapore withholding tax calculator.

What is Singapore withholding tax?

When a Singapore business pays certain types of income to a non-resident, it must deduct a percentage of the payment and pay it to IRAS on the non-resident's behalf. The non-resident receives the balance. The Singapore payer, not the foreign recipient, is legally responsible for withholding, filing Form IR37 and paying the tax on time.

It applies when three things are true: the payee is not tax resident in Singapore, the payment is of a type listed in the Income Tax Act (interest, royalties, certain service fees, equipment rent, director fees and a few others), and the income is sourced in Singapore. Dividends are not on the list. Singapore does not tax dividends paid to foreign shareholders.

The rule most foreign suppliers miss: work done outside Singapore

Service fees are only subject to Singapore withholding tax if the services are performed in Singapore. IRAS's own guidance says that where a non-resident provides services from overseas, for example electronically and without sending staff to Singapore, the services are rendered outside Singapore and withholding tax does not apply.

  • An Indian software agency building an app for a Singapore startup from Bengaluru: no Singapore withholding tax.
  • A Vietnamese design studio working remotely for a Singapore brand: no Singapore withholding tax.
  • A UK consultant who flies in for a two-week workshop in Singapore: the Singapore part is in scope, though the UK treaty usually exempts it if the consultant's firm has no permanent establishment in Singapore. The payer must still file Form IR37 to claim the exemption.

If a project mixes onsite and offshore work, split the fee in the contract and on the invoice. Without a split, IRAS can treat the whole fee as Singapore work.

No US treaty. Singapore's agreements with the United States and Hong Kong cover only shipping and air transport income. A Singapore company paying a US company interest or royalties withholds at the full domestic rates. The US company can usually claim a foreign tax credit at home.

How to claim a treaty rate

  1. Get a Certificate of Residence (CoR) from the payee's home tax authority, covering the year of payment. Without one, use the domestic rate.
  2. Check the payee has no permanent establishment in Singapore. A Singapore branch, office or staff present for long periods can switch off treaty relief for service fees.
  3. Withhold at the treaty rate (or not at all, where the treaty exempts the income).
  4. File Form IR37 on myTax Portal, even when the treaty reduces the tax to zero, and keep the CoR on file in case IRAS asks.

Three worked examples

1. Royalty to a Malaysian company

A Singapore company pays a Malaysian company S$50,000 for the right to use its brand. Domestic rate 10%, Malaysia treaty rate 8%. With a Malaysian CoR, the Singapore company withholds S$4,000 and pays the Malaysian company S$46,000, saving S$1,000 compared with the domestic rate.

2. Onsite consulting from a US firm, net of tax

A US consulting firm sends a team to Singapore and the contract says it must receive S$20,000 "free of any taxes". There is no US treaty, so the 17% rate applies, and because the Singapore company bears the tax, it is grossed up:

  • Gross fee = S$20,000 / (1 - 0.17) = S$24,096.39
  • Withholding tax = S$4,096.39, paid by the Singapore company on top of the S$20,000

Net-of-tax clauses are common in contracts drafted by foreign suppliers. Read them before you sign: they can add 20% or more to the real cost.

3. Shareholder loan from a UAE parent

A Singapore subsidiary pays S$30,000 of interest on a loan from its UAE parent. The Singapore-UAE treaty rate on interest is 0%, so with a UAE CoR nothing is withheld. The subsidiary still files Form IR37 to report the payment and claim the treaty rate. The interest rate itself should be at arm's length; model related-party pricing with our transfer pricing simulator.

Filing deadline and penalties

Withholding tax must be filed (Form IR37) and paid by the 15th of the second month after the date of payment. The date of payment is the earliest of the date the payment is due under the contract, the date it is paid, or the date it is credited to the payee's account. A royalty due on 10 November 2026 must be filed and paid by 15 January 2027.

  • Late payment: a 5% penalty on the unpaid tax.
  • Still unpaid: an additional 1% for each completed month, up to a further 15%.
  • If you forgot to withhold, the Singapore payer still owes the tax and may also face penalties for late filing.

If you are the foreign supplier

  • Say where the work is done in the contract. If it is all offshore, say so; it is the cleanest way to avoid a deduction.
  • Send your Certificate of Residence before the first invoice so your client can apply the treaty rate.
  • Agree who bears the tax. "Fees are exclusive of withholding tax" (client grosses up) and "fees are subject to applicable withholding tax" (you absorb it) lead to very different outcomes.
  • Claim a foreign tax credit at home for Singapore tax withheld. Ask your client for the IRAS withholding tax payment records.

If you own a Singapore company paying overseas

Founders who set up in Singapore from India, Indonesia, Malaysia or the UK often pay management fees, royalties or interest back to a related company at home. Those payments are where withholding tax and transfer pricing meet. Keep a written agreement, price it at arm's length, check whether the work happens in Singapore, and diarise the IR37 deadline every time you pay. Common mistakes

  • Withholding 17% on a remote service performed wholly overseas, where no withholding tax applies.
  • Applying a treaty rate without a Certificate of Residence.
  • Not filing IR37 because the treaty rate is 0%.
  • Assuming a US or Hong Kong supplier gets treaty relief.
  • Agreeing to a net-of-tax clause without budgeting for the gross-up.
  • Treating dividends as subject to withholding tax; they are not.
