Japanese Business Owners and Companies in Singapore (2026): Tax, CFC Rules and How to Structure It

📅 Published 27 Nov 2026 ⏱ 11 min read ✍️ Karman Editorial Team

Quick answer

Japan has some of the highest taxes on owners in the world: inheritance and gift tax up to 55%, a combined top income tax rate of about 55.9%, an exit tax on emigrants with ¥100 million or more in financial assets, and a new minimum tax on ultra-high incomes from 2025. Singapore has no inheritance, gift or capital gains tax, a 24% top personal rate and 17% corporate tax. But Japan's controlled foreign company (CFC) rules use a 20% trigger rate, so a Singapore subsidiary can have its income taxed in Japan unless it runs an active business with real substance. Singapore works best for Japanese companies as a genuine regional headquarters for Southeast Asia, and for owners whose families genuinely relocate and plan around the 10-year inheritance tax rule.

  • Japan inheritance and gift tax: top rate 55%. Japan-situs assets are always taxable.
  • Japanese nationals stay taxable on worldwide assets if decedent or heir lived in Japan within the last 10 years.
  • Exit tax: deemed sale of financial assets on departure if they total ¥100 million or more.
  • Japan CFC trigger rate is 20% (27% for paper companies). Singapore at 17% can fall below it.
  • An active Singapore business with real staff usually passes the CFC active business test.
  • Japan-Singapore tax treaty: dividend withholding as low as 5% for substantial corporate holdings.

Japanese companies have used Singapore as their Southeast Asia base for decades, and more Japanese owners and families are now looking at Singapore for themselves. A shrinking domestic market pushes companies to grow abroad, and Japan's high inheritance and income taxes push families to plan succession early. This guide explains the Japanese rules that matter, what Singapore offers, and where a Singapore company genuinely helps.

The Japanese rules that drive decisions

Inheritance and gift tax: up to 55%

Japan's inheritance tax reaches 55% on the portion of each heir's share above ¥600 million, after a basic exemption of ¥30 million plus ¥6 million per statutory heir. Gift tax also reaches 55%. Since 2024, gifts made in the 7 years before death (up from 3) are added back to the estate.

Moving abroad does not end Japanese inheritance tax quickly:

  • Japan-situs assets, including shares in a Japanese company and Japanese property, are always taxable, whoever and wherever the heirs are.
  • Worldwide assets stay taxable if the decedent or the heir is a Japanese national who had a domicile in Japan within the previous 10 years. Both generations generally need to have lived outside Japan for more than 10 years before foreign assets fall outside Japanese inheritance tax.

Exit tax on departure

Since July 2015, Japan has taxed residents who leave the country holding ¥100 million or more in covered financial assets (such as listed and unlisted shares, funds and some derivatives; cash and real estate are excluded) if they were resident for at least 5 of the last 10 years. The assets are treated as sold at market value on the day you leave. Payment can be deferred for 5 years, extendable to 10, if you appoint a tax agent and provide security, and the tax can be cancelled if you return within the deferral period still holding the assets.

High income and the new minimum tax

Japan's top personal rate is 45%, plus a 2.1% reconstruction surtax and 10% local inhabitant's tax, about 55.9% combined. From 2025, a minimum tax of 22.5% applies to taxable income above ¥330 million, which mainly affects large capital gains. A 2026 reform proposal would lower the threshold and raise the rate from 2027.

Corporate tax

The effective statutory corporate tax rate for a large company in Tokyo is about 31.5%, including a 4% special defence corporate tax from fiscal years starting in 2026.

The catch: Japan's CFC rules

This is the rule most Japanese owners underestimate. If Japanese companies or residents own more than 50% of a foreign company, Japan's controlled foreign company rules can tax a 10%+ Japanese shareholder on its share of that company's income:

  • The general trigger rate is 20%. Singapore's 17% headline rate, and lower effective rates after exemptions, can fall below it.
  • If the Singapore company fails the active business test (for example, it is mainly a holding or IP company without real operations), all of its income can be included in the Japanese parent's or owner's income.
  • "Paper" or "cash box" companies without real substance or management face a higher trigger of 27%.
  • Even an active company can have its passive income (dividends, interest, royalties, some capital gains) included.

In practice, a Singapore company with its own office, employees, local management and a real trading or service business usually passes the active business test. A Singapore holding company with nothing but shares and a bank account usually does not.

Where a Singapore company works for Japanese businesses

1. Regional headquarters for Southeast Asia

Singapore is the natural base for managing subsidiaries in Indonesia, Vietnam, Thailand, Malaysia and the Philippines. A Singapore regional HQ with real regional management, sales, procurement or treasury functions is an active business, earns profits taxed at Singapore rates, and can apply for EDB incentives. See setting up a regional HQ.

2. Trading and distribution

Companies selling Japanese products across Asia, from food and cosmetics to machinery parts, often run trading through Singapore, with access to Singapore's free trade agreements and banks. The trade must be real: contracts, inventory decisions and people in Singapore.

3. Dividends back to Japan

A Japanese parent holding at least 25% of a foreign subsidiary for six months can exclude 95% of dividends received from Japanese tax. Singapore does not tax dividends paid out, and the Japan-Singapore tax treaty limits Japanese withholding on dividends going the other way to 5% for substantial corporate holdings. See our withholding tax calculator.

4. Families who genuinely relocate

For owners who move to Singapore with their families, Singapore income and future growth on new businesses can be taxed at Singapore rates. But Japanese shares remain Japan-situs assets, the exit tax applies on departure, and the 10-year rule keeps worldwide assets in the Japanese inheritance tax net for a decade. This is long-term planning, not a quick fix. Singapore's single family office route is an option for larger families.

Where it does not work

  • A Singapore company owned by a Japanese resident that just holds investments: CFC rules usually tax it in Japan.
  • Moving the shares of your Japanese company into a Singapore company: that is a taxable transfer in Japan.
  • Running the Singapore company from Tokyo: it undermines Singapore tax residence and the active business test.

Setting up a Singapore subsidiary from Japan

  1. Plan the structure with your Japanese adviser: which functions, people and contracts go to Singapore, and how CFC rules apply.
  2. Incorporate the Pte Ltd: at least one director ordinarily resident in Singapore, a registered address and a company secretary. The Japanese parent can own 100%. Registration usually completes in a day. See company formation for non-residents.
  3. Open a bank account with the parent's documents, including certified translations where needed.
  4. Send managers on Employment Passes. Check scores with our COMPASS calculator.
  5. Document transfer pricing between Japan and Singapore.
  6. Get a Certificate of Residence from IRAS to use the Japan-Singapore treaty.

Large groups (EUR 750 million+ revenue) should also note that Japan has applied the Pillar Two income inclusion rule since April 2024, and Singapore's domestic top-up tax brings in-scope Singapore profits to 15%. See Pillar Two and Singapore holding companies.
