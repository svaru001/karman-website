Where to Set Up a Stablecoin or Payments Company in 2026: Singapore vs Hong Kong vs the US

📅 Published 27 Nov 2026 ⏱ 11 min read ✍️ Karman Editorial Team

Quick answer

Stablecoin rules are now real in all three hubs. The US GENIUS Act takes effect on 18 January 2027, and foreign issuers can only sell to US persons if Treasury finds their home regime comparable. Hong Kong's Stablecoins Ordinance has applied since 1 August 2025, and the HKMA granted its first two issuer licences, to HSBC and Anchorpoint, in April 2026, from 36 applicants. Singapore has a 2023 framework for single-currency stablecoins and, in September 2026, proposed a new stablecoin issuance licence under the Payment Services Act. Very few start-ups will become issuers in any of them. Most stablecoin businesses are payments, wallet, on-ramp or infrastructure companies, and for those a Singapore company with a Major Payment Institution licence (or no licence, for pure software) remains one of the strongest set-ups in Asia, provided it serves real customers from Singapore.

  • US: GENIUS Act signed 18 July 2025, effective 18 January 2027. Foreign issuers need a "comparable" home regime.
  • Hong Kong: licensing since 1 August 2025; first two licences in April 2026 from 36 applicants. More will be limited.
  • Singapore: MAS proposed a new stablecoin issuance licence in September 2026 (consultation closed 16 October).
  • Proposed Singapore rules: 100% reserves held on trust, par redemption, no interest to holders.
  • Most founders are not issuers: payments and DPT services in Singapore need an MPI licence.
  • Since 30 June 2025, Singapore firms serving only overseas customers with digital tokens need a DTSP licence, which MAS grants sparingly.

Stablecoins have moved from crypto niche to regulated payments product. The US passed a federal law, Hong Kong issued its first licences to banks, and Singapore's MAS is writing its rules into statute. For founders building on stablecoin rails, whether cross-border B2B payments, treasury tools, wallets or merchant acquiring, the question has changed from "where is it allowed?" to "where can we get licensed and bank-ready?". This guide compares the three main hubs as of November 2026.

United States: the biggest market, the highest bar

The GENIUS Act creates a federal regime for payment stablecoins. It takes effect on the earlier of 18 months after enactment or 120 days after final regulations, which puts it at 18 January 2027. The OCC issued its final rule on 21 October 2026, effective the same day, with a safe harbour for applications that are substantially complete and pending by 18 January 2027.

For a non-US company, the key point is that a foreign issuer can only offer stablecoins to US persons if the US Treasury decides its home country's regime is comparable and the issuer registers with the OCC. That makes the quality of Singapore's and Hong Kong's final rules commercially important.

Hong Kong: open, but selective

Hong Kong's Stablecoins Ordinance has applied since 1 August 2025. The HKMA received 36 applications in its first round and, on 10 April 2026, licensed two: HSBC and Anchorpoint Financial, a joint venture of Standard Chartered, Animoca Brands and HKT. The HKMA has said there is no fixed timetable for more licences and that further licences will be limited. In practice Hong Kong's issuer regime is for banks and well-capitalised consortia, not start-ups.

Singapore: moving from framework to statute

What exists today

MAS finalised its framework in August 2023 for single-currency stablecoins pegged to the Singapore dollar or a G10 currency and issued in Singapore. Requirements include reserve backing, capital and liquidity, and redemption at par within five business days.

What MAS proposed in September 2026

On 1 September 2026, MAS published consultation paper P015-2026 proposing amendments to the Payment Services Act:

  • a new stablecoin issuance licence; only licensees could describe their coin as a MAS-regulated stablecoin;
  • coverage for domestic issuance and for coins issued in Singapore and another jurisdiction, with the foreign issuer under a substantively equivalent regime;
  • 100% reserves held on trust with licensed custodians, and redemption at par;
  • a ban on paying interest to holders, possible holding and issuance caps, and powers to designate systemic stablecoins;
  • case-by-case recognition of a limited number of foreign stablecoins.

The consultation closed on 16 October 2026. Subsidiary legislation and a start date are still to come. Issuers of stablecoins that are not MAS-regulated would generally fall under the existing licensing regime for digital payment token (DPT) services.

Most founders are not issuers

Issuing a stablecoin requires bank-level capital, reserves and compliance. Most companies in this space instead build on top of existing stablecoins. In Singapore, the licence you need depends on what you do:

The DTSP rule matters: Singapore no longer suits "offshore-only" crypto companies that incorporate here but serve no one locally. It does suit companies with a real Singapore business, local staff and customers, and a licence application they can support.

Why Singapore still leads for most stablecoin businesses

  • A licensing path for payments, not just issuance, with clear categories under one Act.
  • Banks and partners experienced with licensed DPT and payment companies.
  • Regional reach: Southeast Asia's B2B cross-border payment corridors are a major stablecoin use case.
  • Tax: 17% corporate tax with exemptions, no capital gains tax in most cases, no tax on dividends to shareholders, and a wide treaty network.
  • Talent: Employment Passes for compliance, risk and engineering hires.

Hong Kong may suit teams whose customers and partners are mainly in Greater China. A US entity is needed for US customers after 18 January 2027 unless your home regime is found comparable. Many groups end up with a Singapore headquarters plus a US entity.

Setting up a stablecoin or payments company in Singapore

  1. Map your flows with a lawyer: who holds funds or tokens, at which point, for which customers. This decides the licence.
  2. Incorporate the Pte Ltd with the right SSIC code and a constitution that suits future investors. See SSIC code search.
  3. Build substance before applying: Singapore-based executive director, compliance officer and AML/CFT framework. MAS assesses people, not paperwork.
  4. Apply to MAS for the relevant licence, budgeting for base capital, security deposits and technology risk requirements.
  5. Open bank accounts: expect detailed due diligence on flows and licensing status.
  6. Keep compliant: annual audit, MAS reporting, plus normal ACRA and IRAS filings. See annual compliance checklist.
